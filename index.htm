01 • Understand the issue

Bullying is a school-climate issue—not just a student-discipline issue.

Bullying generally involves unwanted aggressive behavior, a real or perceived power imbalance, and repetition or a strong likelihood of repetition. Conflict between students is not automatically bullying; leaders should investigate the pattern, power dynamics, impact, and context rather than relying on labels or assumptions.

🧭

Recognize the forms

Physical aggression, verbal harassment, social exclusion or rumor-spreading, and cyberbullying can all harm students. Some behavior is subtle, relational, or hidden from adults.

👀

Look beyond visibility

Bullying can occur in hallways, buses, restrooms, lunch areas, online spaces, group work, and transitions. Student reports and supervision data help reveal patterns.

🤝

Protect belonging

Students need to know that adults will listen, act fairly, protect privacy, and follow up. A safe school is built through everyday interactions, not a poster alone.

Leadership reminder: Never assume a student is “too sensitive,” that teasing is harmless, or that a student’s disability explains away the behavior. Take reports seriously, gather facts, and follow district policy and applicable law.
02 • Connect leadership to practice

Turn leadership principles into visible action.

Fullan’s Leading in a Culture of Change, Chapter 7, focuses on leadership for change. His emphasis on learning, relationships, and building momentum offers a useful frame for bullying prevention: sustainable change takes shared responsibility, ongoing learning, and consistent practice. Whitaker’s leadership message likewise highlights the principal’s influence on people, respect, and school culture.

Fullan • Leadership for change

Build shared ownership

Do not treat prevention as a one-time assembly or a single staff member’s job. Establish a team, use evidence to identify needs, implement a manageable plan, and review whether it is working.

Leader move: Set a recurring meeting to review reports, student voice, supervision gaps, and intervention follow-through.

Whitaker • Respect and example

Model the climate you expect

Students and staff notice what leaders tolerate, praise, and address. Calm, respectful, consistent adult behavior helps make dignity a schoolwide norm.

Leader move: Respond promptly to disrespect, coach adults privately when needed, and recognize staff who protect student dignity.

Knowledge sharing

Teach adults what to do

Give staff common definitions, reporting steps, sample language, and opportunities to rehearse responses. Information alone is not enough; staff need practice and feedback.

Leader move: Use brief scenarios in faculty meetings and revisit procedures during the year.

Coherence

Align systems and messages

Connect the code of conduct, classroom expectations, disability supports, family communication, counseling, and data review so students receive consistent responses.

Leader move: Audit whether the written policy matches what students and adults experience in practice.

03 • Prevent before harm escalates

Five layers of a proactive prevention system

1. Set clear norms

Co-create simple expectations for respect, inclusion, safe disagreement, reporting, and digital behavior. Teach and revisit them—not just post them.

2. Train every adult

Practice how to interrupt harmful behavior, support the targeted student, document facts, report concerns, and avoid public shaming or dismissive comments.

3. Improve supervision

Map incident locations and times. Increase active adult presence in hotspots, transitions, buses, cafeterias, playgrounds, and less-structured settings.

4. Build student voice

Offer safe, accessible ways to report concerns. Use climate surveys, advisory discussions, student leadership, and confidential check-ins to understand what adults may not see.

5. Teach skills and belonging

Explicitly teach empathy, perspective-taking, conflict resolution, help-seeking, bystander action, and inclusive collaboration. Reinforce positive behavior and connection.

6. Partner with families

Explain reporting channels, response steps, and follow-up expectations. Communicate respectfully and protect the privacy of all students involved.

04 • Disability-inclusive prevention

Make sure prevention works for students with disabilities.

Research reviews on bullying and students with disabilities highlight the importance of examining both victimization and perpetration, the school context, and the supports available to students. Disability does not determine whether a student will bully or be bullied. Leaders should avoid stereotypes and assess each situation individually.

Reduce barriers to reporting

  • Offer verbal, written, visual, and assistive-technology reporting options.
  • Use accessible language and check that the student understands the questions.
  • Allow extra processing time and a trusted support person when appropriate.
  • Ask about communication, sensory, mobility, and supervision needs.

Respond without assumptions

  • Interview students in a way that supports communication and dignity.
  • Distinguish disability-related communication or support needs from intentional harmful behavior without excusing harm.
  • Review IEP/504 supports and consult the appropriate team when relevant.
  • Ensure discipline and safety responses follow policy, procedural protections, and individualized needs.
Equity check: Ask whether students with disabilities can participate in prevention lessons, access reporting systems, receive adult supervision, and benefit from follow-up at the same level as their peers.
05 • When a concern is reported

A consistent response protects students and trust.

  1. Stop immediate harm and secure safety.
    Separate students if needed, obtain medical or crisis support when appropriate, and ensure the targeted student is not left unsupported.
  2. Listen, reassure, and document.
    Speak privately with students and relevant witnesses. Record dates, locations, exact reported words/actions, observed facts, and immediate steps. Avoid promising absolute secrecy.
  3. Investigate fairly and promptly.
    Gather information separately, consider power imbalance and repeated behavior, review digital or other evidence lawfully, and follow district reporting procedures.
  4. Apply policy and teach accountability.
    Use proportionate, policy-aligned consequences and interventions. Avoid forced face-to-face mediation when a power imbalance or safety concern makes it inappropriate.
  5. Support all students involved.
    Provide a safety plan and check-ins for the targeted student. Address the behavior and skill needs of the student who caused harm. Consider witnesses and bystanders, too.
  6. Follow up and check for retaliation.
    Set dates to re-check safety, communicate with families as appropriate, monitor recurrence, and adjust supports if the behavior continues.
Use local procedures: This guide is educational and does not replace district policy, state law, mandatory reporting requirements, or emergency procedures. Follow the school system’s official reporting and investigation protocols.

07 • Leader toolkit

Ready-to-use resources

Conversation starters for staff

  • “What would make this behavior a bullying concern rather than a one-time conflict?”
  • “How can we intervene without humiliating a student?”
  • “Which students might face barriers to reporting or being believed?”
  • “What evidence will tell us that our response worked?”

What to track

  • Reports received and time to initial response
  • Locations, times, and behavior patterns
  • Safety check-ins and follow-up completion
  • Repeat incidents and retaliation concerns
  • Student sense of belonging and confidence in adults

Interpret numbers carefully: an increase in reports can reflect greater trust in the reporting system, not necessarily an increase in bullying.

Quick leadership scenario

A student with a disability reports that peers repeatedly mock the way they communicate. A staff member says, “They are just joking.” What should the leader do first?

Why is the best response appropriate?

It avoids minimizing the concern, protects student dignity, begins a fair fact-finding process, and follows established procedures. A disability should never be used to dismiss a report or make assumptions about intent.

How can leaders know whether prevention is working?

Use multiple indicators: student and family feedback, staff confidence, accessibility of reporting, response timeliness, recurrence, safety check-ins, and climate data. Review trends over time and pair quantitative data with student voice.
